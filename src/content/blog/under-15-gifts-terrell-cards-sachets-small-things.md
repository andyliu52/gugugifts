## The Hardest Gift Is the Small One

Fifty dollars is easy. Fifteen is hard.

Under $15 is where most December gifting actually happens — the neighbor, the mail carrier, the bus driver, the twelve people at the office, the cousin you drew in the exchange. And it's where most gifts go wrong, because the obvious options at that price look like what they cost.

Here's what we'd hand you instead.

## Cardthartic Cards — $2 to $14

Forty-four in stock. Cardthartic is an illustrated card line that doesn't do the sentimental thing — the art carries it.

- **Mini cards — $2.** Flower, Sunflower, Red Flower. Small enough to attach to anything.
- **Standard cards — $4.25.** "Higher Number," "One Big Happy," and a rotating rest.
- **Flowery Hello Collection Box — $14.** A boxed set. This is a gift on its own, not a card.

A $2 mini card attached to a $6 item turns two cheap things into one considered thing. That's most of what this post is about.

## Greenleaf Scented Sachets — $1.50

The most useful object in the store at the price. A paper sachet that goes in a drawer, a closet, a car, or a suitcase and scents it for months.

- **Shimmering Snowberry Slim Sachet — $1.38**

Buy six. Tuck one into every card you're already sending. Total spend: nine dollars, and every one of them gets used.

## Three Builds That Work

**The $10 build.** Greenleaf sachet ($1.50) + Cardthartic standard card ($4.25) + a second sachet for the card ($1.50). Comes to about $7 and looks deliberate.

**The $15 build.** OSCOLABO stamp ($10) + mini card ($2) + sachet ($1.50). Under $14, and unlike anything else they'll receive.

**The twelve-coworkers build.** Twelve mini cards ($24) + twelve sachets ($18) = $42 for twelve gifts, wrapped. Three-fifty a person, and not one of them reads as cheap.

That last one is the single most practical thing in this post. People spend $150 on a dozen mediocre small gifts every December. This is better and costs a third.

## The Real Rule

A small gift fails when it looks like the person spent nothing *thinking*. It rarely fails for costing little. Two small objects chosen to go together will beat one larger object chosen at random, essentially every time — and at this price you can afford to pair things.
